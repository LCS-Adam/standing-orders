# That Code Bro Sandbox Life: Code of Conduct

Welcome to the repo, bro. We are here to build cool sandcastles, ship useful code, squash bugs, and avoid becoming the human equivalent of an unhandled exception.

This project runs on one simple rule: **do no harm**. Not "be perfectly cheerful forever," not "pretend every PR comment is a hug," and definitely not "weaponize etiquette like a LinkedIn thought leader." Just don't wreck people, don't wreck the project, and don't make maintainers babysit your main-character arc.

## The Sandbox Pact

### 1. Don't Throw Sand

No harassment, threats, bullying, targeted insults, creepy behavior, spam, sabotage, or drive-by cruelty disguised as "just being honest."

Critique the code. Roast the bug. Side-eye the architecture if you must. But don't go after the person behind the keyboard like you're auditioning for Villain.js.

Bad: "You're an idiot."
Better: "This function mutates global state, which is how demons enter the build."

### 2. Don't Kick Over Other People's Sandcastles

Do not vandalize issues, derail discussions, grief PRs, sneak in malicious code, or turn every thread into your personal debate arena.

We are not here to win the internet. We are here to merge decent code without summoning chaos goblins.

### 3. Leave the Codebase Better Than You Found It

Follow the campsite rule, bro: if you see a mess, improve it. If you make a mess, fix it faster.

That means readable commits, decent comments when needed, tests when reasonable, and no "works on my machine" energy unless your machine is also production, staging, CI, and everyone else's laptop. Spoiler: it is not.

### 4. Assume Bug, Not Supervillain

When someone breaks the build, ships a weird patch, misunderstands an issue, or asks a question that seems obvious, start with the assumption that they are human, not a weekend-destroying cyber-wizard.

Point out the problem clearly. Keep it useful. Skip the dramatic courtroom monologue.

"Hey, this breaks X because Y" is chad behavior.
"Clearly you don't understand software" is clown behavior.

### 5. Disagree Without Becoming a Fork Bomb

Technical disagreement is normal. Passion is fine. Strong opinions are fine. Turning a thread into a 78-comment ego cage match is not fine.

Argue the tradeoff. Bring examples. Share context. Then let maintainers decide. Nobody gets extra merit badges for exhausting everyone into submission.

### 6. Don't Make the Reporter Do Your Job

If someone reports a problem, they are not required to personally duel the offender in the comments like this is Mortal Kombat: Maintainer Edition.

The repo owner or maintainers handle it. The person reporting can provide details, links, screenshots, or context, but they do not have to confront the person directly unless they choose to.

## Reporting Problems

To report a Code of Conduct issue, message the **repo author/owner** privately.

Include whatever you can reasonably provide:

* What happened
* Where it happened, such as issue, PR, discussion, commit, chat, or other project space
* Who was involved
* Links, screenshots, or copied text, if available
* Whether you want your name kept out of the follow-up

Do not write a 90-page legal brief unless you truly need to. Just give enough signal so the maintainer is not debugging social spaghetti with zero logs.

If the repo owner is the problem, use the platform's moderation tools, organization admin route, or another trusted project admin if one exists. Reporting the final boss to the final boss is not always a great architecture pattern, bro.

## How Enforcement Works

A code of conduct with no enforcement is just a README wearing a fake mustache. So here is the actual process.

### 1. The Report Gets Reviewed

The repo owner or maintainers will take reports seriously, even if the situation does not match their personal experience with the person involved.

Sometimes a person has a long pattern. Sometimes one bad incident is enough. Context matters. Receipts help. Vibes alone are not a database.

### 2. Maintainers Gather Context

Before acting, maintainers may review comments, issues, PRs, past behavior, and relevant project history. They may ask follow-up questions.

This is not an invitation for endless debate, character essays, or "actually, the real victim is my ego" counter-lore. The goal is to understand what happened and prevent more damage.

### 3. Maintainers Choose a Response

Depending on severity and pattern, maintainers may take one or more of these actions:

* Ask someone to stop or rephrase
* Edit, hide, or remove harmful comments
* Give a public warning where the behavior happened
* Send a private warning when details are sensitive
* Lock a thread that has become a trash fire
* Temporarily suspend participation
* Permanently remove someone from the project

Permanent bans are not confetti. They are used when the behavior is serious, repeated, hostile, or clearly not going to change.

## The Maintainer Energy

Maintainers should be clear, calm, and firm. No revenge arcs. No favoritism. No "but they write good code so their bad behavior is fine" nonsense.

A 10x engineer who makes everyone else 0.5x is not a genius. That is just a distributed denial-of-vibes attack.

## Final Rule

Be useful. Do less harm. Fix what you break. Respect people's time. Don't be the reason the repo needs a meeting.

Follow that, bro, and your sandbox privileges remain intact. Violate it, and the maintainers may revoke your shovel, your bucket, and your ability to fling sand in this project.
